Abstract

Phase field modelling is a mathematical way for simulating microstructural evolution during
phase transformations and has applications in simulating material processes like phase
transformation, precipitate growth and crack propagation etc. The phase field modelling
adopts diffused interface approach which does not requires tracking of the position of the
interfaces during microstructure evolution and is implicitly expressed by the temporal and
spatial evolution of phase field variables. Phase field modelling approach results in more
accurate temporal evolution of microstructure as compared to traditional methods like Sharp
Interface Method. Phase field modelling can simulate 2D as well as 3D simulations. In this
work phase field method is used to study the dendritic crystal growth in pure melt as well as
binary alloy. The modelling of dendrite growth is described using a set of partial differential
equations which were solved using explicit Finite Difference Method and simple forward
Euler time marching scheme.
Phase field modelling of solidification of pure Ni was studied and results were compared
with literature. For initial conditions along without undercooling a fine solid layer is added
to the domain walls. It is seen that as the time passes, interface became unstable and grew
uniformly into the domain. On the other hand, on applying no heat flux condition and
introducing supercooling, selective branches were witnessed into the domain. Simulations
were also performed for different seeding configurations. Along with this the effect of
undercooling on the dendrite tip velocity and dendrite arm spacing was studied and results
were compared with experimental data. The phase field approach was also applied to simulate
the dendrite growth in a Al-Zn alloy.

Introduction
The microstructure can be defined as spatial organization of various phases and potential
flaws within a system, which exhibit distinct compositional and/or structural characteristics.
The physical and mechanical properties of the material depends on the size, shape and
orientations of the grains and domains. hence it is very important to gain an insight on the
microstructure. evolution. The microstructure evolution involves large diversity of complex
processes hence. it requires an extensive experimental and theoretical research to study the
mechanism of microstructure evolution. Due to advancement in the technology with help of
mathematics it is. now possible to predict and model the microstructure properties. Phase
Field Modelling or Phase Field Method is a modern way for simulating complex processes
in material science such as solidification ,precipitate growth and grain growth etc.

1.1

Phase Field Modelling

Phase Field Modelling is mathematical way of simulating and analysing evolution of phase
boundary and microstructure in materials. This technique is quite modern but nowadays it is
widely used in material science and engineering field. In phase field modelling microstructure
is expressed in terms of phase field variables which are continuous functions in space and
time. The phase field modelling is based on the "diffused interface" approach. In diffused
interface approach the interface between two domains is assumed to be of finite thickness. In
this narrow region the values of phase field variables vary gradually between the values in
the neighbouring domain.
In phase field method different phases present in the system are described in terms of
phase field variables. The values field variables are remained constant in the bulk phases, and
smoothly varies across interfaces from one value to another. By taking a variational derivative

of free energy functional the governing equations for the phase-field and its coupling to
relevant thermodynamic state variables such as temperature and composition are obtained.
The phase-field method involves representing a system using one or more field variables
(known as order parameters). The field variables have fixed values in their respective
bulk phases and smoothly change between phases across interfaces with a characteristic
thickness of ∆. By performing a variational derivative of the free energy functional, the
governing equations for the phase-field can be obtained along with its coupling to relevant
thermodynamic state variables such as temperature and composition.
Phase field variables are continuous functions in time and spatial coordinates. Generally,
phase field variables can be divided into two categories : 1) Conserved variables 2) Non
conserved variables. Common Conserved variables are local concentration, composition
etc. In the phase field simulation of solid sintering process density is used as a conserved
phase field variable. Non conserved variables are fictious variables introduced in phase field
modelling to define particular phase or crystal structure. For example, order parameter () is
the order parameter used in grain growth simulation which represents individual grain. In
the solidification non conserved variable called phase field variable (φ ) is used. In the solid
phase φ takes value equal to one and in the liquid phase it takes value equal to one. Figure
1.1 shows the representation of phase field variable. At the interface phase field φ varies
gradually from zero to one.

Fig. 1.1 Representation of Phase field variable (Kobayashi et. al.1993)[16]
The temporal and spatial evolution of phase field variables is given by set of partial
differential equations which describes the evolution of microstructure. These differential
equations are derived based on the general thermodynamics and kinetics principles which
includes different driving forces for microstructure evolution such as reduction in bulk

1.2

Advantages of Phase Field Modelling

Phase field method is based on diffused interface approach. Prior to phase field modelling the
problems related phase transformation were solved using sharp interface model proposed by
Stephan in 1889. In this conventional modelling technique unlike diffused interface approach
the interface between the domains was sharp.(Figure 1.2)

Fig. 1.2 (a)Diffused interface (b)Sharp interface approach[1]

In sharp interface model for each phase different differential equations needs to be solved,
Along with that boundary condition flux conservation condition needs to be applied at the
interface. For applying boundary conditions, it requires constant tracking of the interface
which is computationally difficult. Phase field modelling overcomes this big disadvantage,
because in phase field modelling there is no need to track the interface also set of differential
equations are satisfied all over the domain irrespective of phases.
There are several other advantages of using phase field modelling as a computational
technique for simulating microstructures in materials, They are as follows:
• Simulating complex microstructures: Phase field modelling can simulate the evolution
of complex microstructures such as dendritic, eutectic, and peritectic structures, which
are difficult to model using other techniques.
• Multiscale modelling: Phase field modelling can be used to study microstructure evolution at different length and time scales, making it a powerful tool for understanding
the fundamental mechanisms that control the behaviour of materials under different
conditions.

• Incorporating thermodynamics: Phase field modelling takes into account the thermodynamic properties of materials such as free energy and mobility of different phases,
which is important for predicting the evolution of microstructures in materials.
• 3D modelling: Phase field modelling can simulate microstructures in three dimensions,
which is important for accurately predicting the behaviour of materials.
• Cost-effective: Phase field modelling is cost-effective because it eliminates the need
for expensive experiments and trial-and-error approaches in material design.

2.1

Solidification in metals

Nucleation
Nucleation is fundamental process in the solidification of metals. The process of nucleation
involves the formation of a small, stable cluster of atoms or molecules, which can then act as
a seed for further crystal growth. The size and stability of these nuclei depend on a variety of
factors, including the temperature and pressure of the system, the composition of the liquid
or gas, and the presence of impurities or defects.
Nucleation can be of two types i.e. homogenous and heterogeneous. Homogeneous
nucleation is the occurred, during the general movement within the liquid, ordered groups of
atoms forming small zones of higher than average density. The driving force for the growth
of the nucleus is the reduction in the free energy. The free energy is composed of both
volume free energy and interfacial free energies. For a spherical particle volume free energy
can be given as:

The radius of the particle is denoted by r and the bulk free energy change per unit volume
is represented by ∆Gv . The interfacial free energy, which is proportional to the surface area
of nuclei, can be expressed as:
∆G = 4πr2 γ[5]
For a spherical surface, γ represents the interfacial free energy per unit area. As depicted
in Figure 2.1, the total free energy change is positive at very small values of r but becomes
negative as r increases. The critical radius is the radius at which the peak positive value of
∆G occurs. Beyond this radius, growth can occur with a decrease in free energy.

Fig. 2.1 Change in free energy ∆G during soldification process [5]

The critical radius serves as a barrier for nucleus formation. Although small nuclei are
unstable, some of them become stable and grow when they reach the critical radius. Those
that fail to reach it melt back into the liquid.
Heterogeneous nucleation takes place when a foreign particle present in the melt or the
wall of container provides the initial growth interface. This type of nucleation is also referred
to as nucleation on the walls of the mold. In order for nucleation to take place, the foreign
surface must be wetted by the metal, resulting in a low contact angle, and must possess some
structural affinity with the crystalline solid.
Homogeneous nucleation generally requires considerable undercooling. However, in
practical settings, heterogeneous nucleation is preferred due to the presence of foreign
particles or container walls. Consequently, solidification can occur with little undercooling.

Growth
The ultimate crystallographic structure of the solid is determined by the growth processes
that occur after nucleation. The way in which individual grains and the mass of solid grow
depends on the temperature and composition (in the case of alloy) present in the domain.
In pure metal one can think , if the temperature is decrease uniformly, the liquid would
undergo random nucleation. However, in practice, temperature gradients arise, resulting in
nucleation starting at the cooler surface of the mould, and growth towards the centre of the
casting (see Figure 2.2). As atoms deposit themselves onto the existing crystals’ lattice, a
nearly flat interface moves forward. The growth occurs through the extension of close-packed
atomic planes, resulting in a small irregular structure on the growth interface [5]. However,
macroscopically, the interface is usually smooth and non-crystallographic in most metals.
This series of events leads to the formation of the characteristic columnar structure.

Fig. 2.2 Development of columnar grain structure

The direction of heat extraction during solidification is opposite to that of growth, resulting
in a positive thermal gradient at the solid-liquid interface. As the temperature of growth
inside the casting progresses, the interface advances gradually. However, if the latent heat
of crystallization is insufficient to reverse the direction of heat flow in the liquid near the
interface, freezing occurs.
For alloys, solute rejection at the interface leads to the formation of a concentration
gradient on the liquid side of the interface, as depicted in Figure 2.3a. This concentration
gradient causes corresponding variation in the freezing temperature, as shown in Figure 2.3b.
There is unique freezing temperature (TE ) for each value of concentration. When the local

2.2

Dendritic Growth

If the liquid adjacent to the solid-liquid interface experiences undercooling, any existing
protrusion on the solid surface becomes stable and serves as a centre for selective growth.
This leads to the formation of dendritic structures in the undercooled zone, where the
advancement of the interface is impeded by either the solute barrier or latent heat. The
preferred growth at the edge or corner of an existing crystal produces the primary axis of
the dendrite. Additionally, the accumulation of solute or latent heat restricts the lateral
expansion of the primary branch, giving rise to secondary and tertiary branches through the

Dendrites are a significant feature in many material processing techniques, often observed

2.3

Review on phase field models used for dendritic solidification

Solidification modelling techniques have a long history dating back to the 19th century.
Stephan developed the sharp interface formulation in 1889 while studying ice formation
in arctic regions. In his formulation he has expressed the diffusion process in solid and
liquid phase and he took account the latent heat generation during solidification. and This
formulation, which expresses the diffusion process in both the solid and liquid bulk and the
generation of latent heat at the interface, remains the traditional mathematical model for

12

Literature Review

solidification. The Gibbs-Thomson law also explains the variation in melting point caused by
interface curvature. In this approach, the location of the interface must be explicitly tracked,
and boundary conditions must be applied there.
The diffuse interface approach can also be traced back to van der Waals’ early studies
in 1893. However, this work was generally ignored until Rawlinson translated it in 1979[2].
The phase-field equations were developed based on Cahn and Hilliard’s 1958 research on
spinodal decomposition. Here, the order parameter was composition, and the diffusion
equation was derived using a Ginzburg-Landau free energy functional. Allen and Cahn used
a non-conserved order parameter to investigate antiphase boundary motion in the context of
the order-disorder transition in 1979. Both the Allen-Cahn and Cahn-Hilliard equations are
essential for the phase-field modelling of solidification processes.

2.3.1

Solidification of a pure material

Based on Halperin’s work, Fix and Langer introduced the concept of using a continuous
non-conserved order parameter to differentiate between two coexisting phases, This approach
was further explored by several researchers and they proposed their own models for diffuse
interfaces in the 1980s. In 1990, Penrose and Fife established a framework for deriving phasefield equations in a thermodynamically consistent manner from an entropy functional[7]. It
was not until 1991 when Kobayashi presented the first dendrite images obtained using the
Langer method, which marked the beginning of the flourishing of phase-field modelling of
solidification. To ensure positive local entropy production, Penrose and Fife emphasized the
necessity of postulating temperature and phase-field equations together. Wang later provided
a thermodynamically consistent phase-field model that guarantees positive entropy production
and is computationally tractable[8]. However, quantitative results using phenomenological
approximations to define phase-field equations are not generally reliable, and it is crucial to
consider interface width that is smaller than the capillary length for obtaining such results.
In 1998, Karma and Rappel proposed the thin interface limit for phase-field models, which
allowed for quantitative analysis of dendritic growth at low undercoolings by accommodating
larger interface widths.

2.3.2

Solidification of binary alloys

After Kobayashi successfully demonstrated dendrites for pure substances, phase field models
for alloys began to develop. In 1993 Wheeler recast the model equations of Kobayashi to
include solute atoms and solute diffusion and developed the WBM model for binary alloys.

2.4 Mathematical methods used in phase field modelling

13

The WBM model used a free energy functional, treating the interface as a thin but of finite
thickness region where field variables like composition vary smoothly.[10]
Warren and Boettinger used the entropy functional to derive the phase field equations.
Their model can be considered as extension to the WBM model. They were able to predict
and show physically relevant dendritic patterns. Beckermann proposed the "two-phase"
approach, treating the interface as a mixture of two phases with separate concentration fields.
Kim introduced the KKS model with two separate concentration fields for each phase.[12]
In 2001 Loginova coupled thermal diffusion equation with phase field equations to give a
non-isothermal solidification model for binary alloys. He considered both solute and thermal
diffusion and solved the equations using implicit finite element methods.[13] Almgren demonstrated anomalous effects when thermal conductivities were unequal, leading to Karma’s
idea of the anti-trapping current. In 2001 Karma proposed ani trapping current formulation.
This was extended by Kim in 2007 to give phase field modelling with antitrapping current
formulation for the multicomponent systems.[14-15] Tönhardt and Amberg treated solid and
liquid phases as Newtonian fluids, while Beckermann introduced melt convection into the
phase-field model. Anderson derived a phase-field model of a pure substance with convection
and analysed the sharp interface limit. However, introducing convection into the melt is
challenging due to the various time and length scales involved and the need for complex
computational methods and adaptive grids.[2]

2.4

Mathematical methods used in phase field modelling

In phase field modelling the main governing equations are non linear partial differential
equations. Implicit and explicit methods are two common approaches for numerically solving
partial differential equations (PDEs). The key difference between the two methods is in how
they handle the time-dependent terms in the PDEs.
In an explicit method, the time derivative term is approximated using a finite difference
formula that only involves the values of the solution at the current time step. This means that
the solution at the next time step can be computed explicitly as a function of the current time
step. Explicit methods are relatively easy to implement and can be computationally efficient,
but they require that the time step be small enough to ensure numerical stability. In general,
explicit methods are used for problems with smooth solutions and short simulation times.
In contrast, in an implicit method, the time derivative term is approximated using a finite
difference formula that involves the values of the solution at both the current and next time
steps. This leads to a system of nonlinear algebraic equations that must be solved at each
time step. Implicit methods are more computationally expensive than explicit methods, but

14

Literature Review

they are also more stable and can handle larger time steps. Implicit methods are typically
used for problems with stiff solutions, long simulation times, or nonlinear behaviour.
In phase field modelling the numerical techniques are used in the time and spatial
discretization of PDEs. The spatial discretization of PDEs is done using following three
methods.
1. Finite difference method
This is a numerical method that approximates derivatives using the differences between
function values at discrete points. The method is simple to implement, but it can be
computationally expensive when dealing with large systems.
2. Finite element method
Finite element method is similar to the finite difference method. In this method solution
of PDSs is approximated using a mesh of small elements. The method can be used to
solve complex geometries and is more computationally efficient than finite difference
methods for large systems.
3. Fourier spectral method
Fourier spectral method is a numerical method that represents the solution of PDEs as
a sum of Fourier series. The method is highly accurate and can be computationally
efficient for smooth functions, but it can be less accurate for functions with discontinuities.
4. Phase field crystal method

The Phase-field crystal (PFC) method is a multiscale simulation technique that connects
classical molecular dynamics (MD) simulations with phase-field methods discussed
above. It was developed by Elder. The Phase field crystal method uses an order
parameter that defines the local-time-averaged atomic number density, allowing for the
production of crystal lattice periodicity. Any perturbations or lattice defects increase the
free energy, thereby providing information that was previously only accessible through
atomistic simulations. Phase field crystal method can simulate various atomistic events
in larger spatial and temporal dimensions. The PFC has many advantages over current
molecular dynamics simulation techniques hence it has become a popular simulation
approach.[5]

Parallel Implementation (MPI)

The written codes comprised of a large number of iterations and for loops, leading to
significant run time. To address this issue and achieve faster results, parallel computing
was employed. Parallel computing refers to the execution of large tasks simultaneously by
dividing them into smaller subtasks that can be executed concurrently on multiple processors.
The aim of parallel computing is to achieve high computational performance, increase speed,
and solve large and complex problems that would be impractical or impossible to solve on a
single processor or computer.
Parallel computing can be achieved using various techniques, such as shared-memory
parallelism, distributed-memory parallelism, hybrid parallelism, and graphics processing unit
(GPU) computing. In the study OpenMP and MPI were used for parallel implementation
on CPUs. OpenMP is an Application Programming Interface that supports multi-platform
shared memory multiprocessing programming. It allows a programmer to parallelize a
program by dividing the computation among multiple threads that run on a shared-memory
system. On the other hand, MPI i.e., message passing interface comes under distributed
memory model. MPI is a standard protocol used for inter-process communication in parallel
computing. It provides a set of functions and routines that enable communication between
multiple processes running in parallel on separate computing nodes.

additional cells are not part of the original array but are used to help with computations
involving the boundary conditions of the array. Ghost cells were exchanged between the
processors using (mpi_isend) and (mpi_recv) routines.

domain, a virtual topology can be created based on which the global domain is decomposed

4.4.3

MPI Communication

The algorithms implemented in this study assigned one processor as the master node and the
rest as slaves. While the gradients and Laplacian were computed on all processors, the time
integration was performed on the master node alone. Thus, it was necessary to transfer data
such as matrices, including φ and temperature, from the master node to the slaves and back,
respectively. This was accomplished using MPI routines such as mpi_send and mpi_recv.

4.4.4

Issues faced during parallel implementation

The C codes prepared for the phase field simulation of binary alloys were implemented on
CPU cluster. For parallel implementation earlier OpenMP was used. But the runtime for the
code was still very high. Later MPICH which is an implementation of the Message Passing
Interface (MPI) was used for writing the parallel codes. The run time for the codes was
coming higher than 2 days due to extra time taken in sending data between processors. At
last Hybrid parallelism was chosen for parallel implementation. Hybrid parallelism refers to
the use of both shared-memory parallelism and distributed-memory parallelism in the same
program or application. In other words, it combines the benefits of parallel programming on
shared-memory systems with those of parallel programming on distributed-memory systems.
So codes were prepared by choosing hybrid approach i.e. combining MPICH and OpenMP
routines. The issue about run time was still occurring because number of loops were large
and limit on cluster was of two days only. Hence it is important to write code such way that
it is optimized for the parallel implementation.

Phase field simulation of Al-Zn alloy

The phase field modelling was applied to simulate the dendrite growth in Al-Zn alloy. In alloy
solidification driving force is both latent heat generation and constitutional supercooling.
Hence while modelling one has to include both solute transfer and heat transfer. In the
early phase of the work non-isothermal solidification approach was followed, in which the
temperature of the domain was changing. This was achieved by coupling thermal diffusion
equation (eq. 3.4) with Allen Cahn and Cahn Hilliard equations. The PDEs were found be
very steep hence the numerical solution was not stable. The main issues were that in alloy
solidification heat transfer and solute transfer take place at different time scales. Usually heat
transfer takes place faster than solute transfer. In most cases, the ratio of thermal (DT ) and
solute (D) diffusivities (Lewis number=DT /D) ranges between 104 to 102 . This creates very
steep thermal gradient in the solid liquid interface. So, for applying explicit finite difference
method and to get numerically stable solution the grid spacing must be very small. This
increases the run time of the program.

The dendrite tip velocity refers to the speed at which dendritic growth occurs during solidification or crystallization processes. The dendrite tip velocity reflects the rate at which the

between the solid and liquid interface increases which increases the rate of solidification
i.e. the dendrite tip velocity. The result obtained from the simulation can be compared with
experimental data available for pure Ni. Figure 5.9b shows the experimental tip velocity
values are almost matching with the results obtained simulation.

Effect of superccoling on dendrite arm spacing

Dendrite arm spacing refers to the average distance between adjacent dendritic arms in a
solidified material. It is a measure of the spacing or distance between individual branches of
dendrites that form during the solidification process.
The dendrite arm spacing significantly influences the microstructure of the solidified
material. Finer dendrite arm spacing corresponds to a higher number of dendritic branches
and a greater surface area of the solid-liquid interface. This fine microstructure can provide
enhanced mechanical properties, such as increased strength and improved fatigue resistance.
On the other hand, coarser dendrite arm spacing results in fewer dendritic branches and a
larger spacing between them, which may lead to reduced mechanical properties.

Effect of phase field mobility

The phase field mobility Mφ is a parameter that describes how quickly the interface between
two phases in a material can move. The growth of the dendrite is directly depends on
the phase field mobility. In the kobayashi model phase field mobility is incorporated by a
parameter τ and is defined as τ = 1/Mφ . To study effect Mφ on the growth rate of dendrite,
the parameter τ in the model is varied from 0.0003-0.005. Other parameters were fixed.
Dimensionless latent heat K was kept equal to 2.
The figure 5.20 shows that, for small values of τ the dendrite grows faster. From the
figure 5.20a and figure 5.20c it can be seen that growth process is much faster for τ =0.0003
compared to τ equal to 0.0005. From this one can infer that as phase mobilty increases
(i.e. τ decreases) atoms take lesser time to retain their equilibrium position and solid liquid
interface movement is faster.

Conclusion
Present study focuses on the phase field modelling of dendritic solidification process. The
phase of the system was expressed using a non conserved variable φ which is continuous
in space and time. The model formulation was done for both pure metal as well as binary
alloy. The numerical scheme was based on the explicit finite difference method for spatial
discretization and simple forward Euler time marching scheme was utilized. For the phase
field simulations algorithms were written in C and MATLAB. Visualization of result was
done in MATLAB.
The effect of thermal supercooling on the dendrite growth was studied using phase
field methods. The variation in the dendrite tip velocity and dendrite arm spacing with
supercooling was studied and results were compared with experimental data.
The simulation of dendritic solidification pure Ni was studied for different types of
seeding configurations. Along with that the effect of model parameters such as latent heat,
strength of anisotropy and phase field mobility on the shape and growth of the dendrite was
studied.
The dendrite growth in Al-Zn alloy was simulated by coupling phase field equation with
Cahn Hilliard equation and by assuming temperature of the domain remain constant. This is
because during solidification heat transfer occurs faster than mass transfer, hence it is very
difficult to couple temperature and solute diffusion equations and to obtain numerically stable
solution using explicit finite difference method. From the study it is inferred that to solve
non isothermal solidification of binary alloy model one should prefer implicit methods over
explicit methods. But while applying implicit methods it is important to account nonlinearity
in equations and extra computational load on computing platforms.
The phase field simulation of binary alloy was performed with cluster computing platform
(CPU) available in the institute, The high run time of the code was main problem faced during
the simulation. To rectify the problem the different tactics were tried to bring run time under

66

two days which is the maximum limit to run a code on cluster. To achieve well developed
dendrites (secondary and ternary branching) it is important to optimize the algorithm such
that the run time will come down and computational efficiency will increase.
